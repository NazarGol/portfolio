European Password Manager Shares Origins and Updates With State-Certified Russian Firm

Investigation 17 July 2026 StateWatch → OCCRP →

A password manager presenting itself as European, operating under Russian jurisdiction, holding an FSB cryptographic licence — and installed inside EU public-sector organisations.

Passwork markets itself on European values and a European corporate identity. The investigation established that the product shares origins and update infrastructure with a Russian legal entity that holds a licence from Russia's Federal Security Service for work involving cryptographic tools, along with further licences from FSTEC, the federal technical and export control service. Those licences are what allow a company to sell into Russian state and state-owned customers. They also carry obligations: under Russian law a licensed operator can be required to provide access to data in its possession.

Its customer base ran in both directions. On one side, government institutions, public authorities and universities across Europe. On the other, Russian companies under sanction. The question the investigation puts is not whether a breach has occurred but who holds the keys — a systemic-risk argument rather than an incident report, which is the harder and more useful kind to make.

The UK has designated none of them

Analysis 2026 Full brief →

97 Russian companies, institutes and state structures developing AI for military, surveillance and information-control applications, each checked against four sanctions regimes.

Sanctions policy has tracked hardware — components, microelectronics, supply chains — because goods are legible to a goods-based regime. The developer layer writing the targeting, recognition, monitoring and censorship software has been tracked much less. I wanted to know how much less, and where exactly the holes are.

62% of the 97 carry no Western designation at all. 41% carry none in any of the four jurisdictions. 20 are Ukraine-listed with no Western follow-through — already assessed by the state fighting the war, with the evidentiary work done and published, and untouched by any Western regime.

The counter-disinformation cut

Fifteen entities build media monitoring, censorship and cyber-operations tooling — the systems that detect, characterise and act on the information environment. US coverage is comparatively strong here at 7 of 15, its best ratio of any cluster. The UK's is zero. Several are marketed openly to government buyers, and at least one claims publicly to forecast public reaction to state initiatives and to identify and "work with" difficult authors and opinion leaders. That is the point at which media monitoring stops being analytics.

Records

Every entity carries a registry number (OGRN), a tax number (INN), addresses in Russian, Ukrainian and English, a sourced designation justification, and an explicit listed or not-listed status per jurisdiction. Recording OGRN and INN rather than names is the load-bearing choice: Russian corporate names are unstable and routinely re-registered, but a registration number survives renaming and is what makes an entity matchable against a sanctions list or a procurement record.

The brief publishes the finding, the cluster taxonomy, the record schema and three fully worked examples — all three drawn only from entities already under public designation, so nothing is disclosed that is not already a published listing. The 97-entity dataset itself is held privately. Full dataset available on request.

Sources

Entity set
Russian-language open sources: company product documentation, state and state-adjacent media, procurement records, technology-park residency lists, university laboratory pages, independent investigative reporting
Designation
Checked per entity against the published lists of all four regimes, recorded as an explicit binary; no jurisdiction inferred from another
Caveat
Designation status is a snapshot; 97 is a floor on the sector, not a census of it

Pro-Russian information network in Moldova

Analysis May 2026 Repository →

Within a fixed perimeter of 28 Telegram channels pushing pro-Russian and anti-Western content in Moldova, does the observable structure look organic — or does it show coordination?

27 channels indexed of 28 targeted, 23,110 messages, 424 endorsement links across 164 unique channel pairs, four clusters mapped. Collection ran through a batch script driving GroupInt across every target — 500 messages each, endorsement extraction, flood-wait handling, failures logged and skipped rather than aborting — into a Neo4j graph. Edges are ENDORSES relations extracted from @username and t.me/ links in message text.

A channel cited by ideologically distinct clusters that cites almost nobody in return is the network's shared authority — the structural signature separating a coordinated network from an organic one.

Coordination indicators

Three, weighted differently rather than collapsed into a single claim. Byte-identical texts published on the same day by @e_tiraspol and @pridnestrovec, spanning weather bulletins, Russian-passport queue notices and utility outages. Four or more channels framing the 27 May gas VAT decision within hours of each other, in near-identical wording, as proof of betrayal rather than as fiscal policy. And a narrow shared set of externally cited channels appearing across otherwise unconnected clusters.

Identical text and synchronised timing are consistent with coordination. They are also consistent with a shared press-service feed. The report says which is which for each pattern rather than collapsing them into one finding.

Sources and limits

Perimeter
The 28-channel set was defined in advance. This describes structure inside a boundary, not a network discovered from scratch

ZhBK-185

Sample report June 2026 Full report →

Two Ukrainian reinforced-concrete plants in occupied Zaporizhzhia oblast, seized and now producing anti-tank obstacles — traced into the Russian corporate network operating them.

ZhBK-185 is not a company. It is a brand operated across several legal entities, which is why the obvious question — what are its finances? — has no honest single answer. The plants at Berdiansk and Melitopol existed before the occupation. The work was to identify what they were, what they became, and whether the same people run them.

Management continuity across the seizure

At Melitopol, the person who was the long-standing director of the pre-war Ukrainian company is now general director of the Russian successor entity, appointed June 2023, holding a founder's stake of approximately 20.5%. The plant did not merely change hands: its management stayed in place and acquired equity in the vehicle that took it. That is a materially different fact from a seizure run by outside operators.

At Berdiansk the pattern differs. Pre-war ownership was linked to the family of a Ukrainian MP; the current director of the Russian entity operating at the original plant address is a former deputy mayor of the city and a known political associate of that same MP. The pre-war political network around the asset is the network now running it.

Method

Identify the pre-war entity from Ukrainian registry data, then find the successor in Russian registries by physical address rather than by name — the address survives the renaming — then check whether the people are the same. Names change, corporate forms change, jurisdictions change; addresses and registry numbers persist.

Individuals are anonymised by role throughout. Corporate registry identifiers are retained: companies operating in occupied territory are legitimate subjects of open-source research, and the registry numbers are the evidence. Personal-profile links have been removed.

Economies of the Aftermath

Investigation Undated Anti-War Coalition Journal →

On economic transformation in post-violence environments, taking Mariupol as its case.

The argument is that systematic infrastructure destruction is not merely damage preceding reconstruction — it is the precondition that makes a new round of economic colonisation possible. What is destroyed is not only physical plant but the arrangements that let an economy function on its own terms: labour relations, market access, the connective infrastructure between them.

The piece proposes reading such economies as an architecture of gaps — disrupted labour, fractured markets, severed infrastructure — rather than as sites simply awaiting reconstruction. The distinction matters because the two readings imply different questions. A site awaiting reconstruction invites asking when rebuilding starts. An architecture of gaps invites asking who the gaps are for.

At the break: Territorialization of Arctic

Investigation Undated Published version →

Russia's transformation of the Arctic through extractivist infrastructure, analysed across four case studies using open-source tools.

The four studies are the Prirazlomnaya oil platform — the first extraction installation on the Arctic shelf — the North Sea Passage, a transarctic fibre-optic cable, and military expansion along the northern border. Taken together they describe a single process rather than four separate developments: as accelerating ice loss opens the region, Russia converts new physical access into infrastructural control.

The strategic reading is that the Arctic's spatial and economic networks are being reoriented toward East Asia. Extraction, shipping route, data cable and garrison are the four layers of the same claim — and each is separately visible in open sources, which is what makes the process traceable without privileged access.
