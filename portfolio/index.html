Passwork — an FSB-licensed password manager in EU government systems

Investigation 17 July 2026 StateWatch → OCCRP →

The question

Passwork sells itself as European software and is installed inside European public-sector organisations. Nobody had established whether the corporate reality behind the product matched the marketing — and if it did not, what that would mean for the institutions running it.

Method

Corporate records across jurisdictions, cryptographic and technical licensing registries, the product's own update infrastructure, and published client lists — cross-checked against sanctions designations. A cross-border effort with OCCRP journalists and StateWatch; my role was contributing research.

Finding

The product shares origins and update infrastructure with a Russian legal entity holding an FSB cryptographic licence obtained in 2025, alongside further FSTEC licences from 2024. Its published client list ran in both directions at once: 5 named European public institutions — Germany's Federal Ministry for Research, the Government of Ireland, the Hildesheim District Administration, the University of Zurich and TU Dresden — and 4 sanctioned Russian companies, Gazprom Neft, Aeroflot, Metrovagonmash and Rusal.

Why it matters

An FSB licence is not only a permission, it is an obligation. Under Russian law a licensed operator can be compelled to provide access to data in its possession, through Federal Law 144-FZ, Law 149-FZ and the SORM framework. The exposure is structural rather than incidental: it does not depend on a breach having occurred.

The question the investigation puts is not whether passwords have been accessed but who holds the keys — a systemic-risk argument, which is the harder and more durable kind to make.

The UK has designated none of them

Analysis 2026 Full brief →

The question

Sanctions policy has tracked hardware — components, microelectronics, supply chains — because goods are legible to a goods-based regime. The developer layer writing the targeting, recognition, monitoring and censorship software has been tracked much less. How much less, and where exactly are the holes?

Method

97 entities identified from Russian-language open sources — product documentation, procurement records, technology-park residency lists, university laboratory pages, independent investigative reporting. Inclusion requires a sourced link to a military, surveillance or information-control application, not merely that the entity works on AI.

Each record carries registry number (OGRN), tax number (INN), addresses in three languages, a sourced designation justification and an explicit listed / not-listed status per jurisdiction. Registry numbers rather than names are the load-bearing choice: Russian corporate names are unstable and routinely re-registered, but a registration number survives renaming.

Finding

Ukraine has designated 47, the United States 31, the European Union 21 — and the United Kingdom none of the 97. 62% carry no Western designation at all, 41% none in any of the four jurisdictions, and 20 are Ukraine-listed with no Western follow-through.

Why it matters

A zero is a different kind of number from a low number. Pro-Russian information network in Moldova

Analysis May 2026 Repository →

The question

Within a fixed perimeter of 28 Telegram channels pushing pro-Russian and anti-Western content in Moldova, does the observable structure look organic — or does it show coordination? The perimeter was defined in advance, so the question is about structure inside a boundary, not about discovering a network from scratch.

Method

A batch script drove GroupInt across every target — 500 messages each, endorsement extraction, flood-wait handling, failures logged and skipped rather than aborting the run — into a Neo4j graph. Edges are ENDORSES relations parsed from @username and t.me/ links in message text. Degree metrics in Cypher; clustering is an analyst's reading, since the Neo4j GDS plugin was unavailable.

Finding

27 channels indexed of 28 targeted, 23,110 messages, 424 endorsement links across 164 unique channel pairs, 4 clusters. @primulinmd receives 83 endorsements and sends 8; KP Moldova sends 129 and receives none.

Three coordination indicators, weighted separately rather than collapsed: byte-identical texts published the same day by two Transnistrian channels; four or more channels framing the 27 May gas VAT decision within hours in near-identical wording; and a narrow shared set of externally cited channels across otherwise unconnected clusters.

Why it matters

A channel cited by ideologically distinct clusters that cites almost nobody in return is the network's shared authority. That degree asymmetry is a structural signature — it does not depend on reading the content, which makes it cheap to compute and hard to disguise.

The honest limit is stated with it: identical text and synchronised timing are consistent with coordination, and equally consistent with a shared press-service feed. The report says which is which for each pattern rather than collapsing them into one claim.

ZhBK-185

Sample report June 2026 Full report →

The question

ZhBK-185 produces anti-tank obstacles in occupied Zaporizhzhia oblast, at sites in Berdiansk and Melitopol. Those are not new-build factories — they are Ukrainian reinforced-concrete plants that existed before the occupation. Which plants were captured, and is the operation run by the same people who ran them before?

Method

Identify the pre-war entity from Ukrainian registry data, then find the successor in Russian registries by physical address rather than by name — the address survives the renaming — then compare directorships across the two systems. Names change, corporate forms change, jurisdictions change; addresses and registry numbers persist.

Finding

Two plants, five linked legal entities, and management continuity across the seizure. At Melitopol the long-standing director of the pre-war Ukrainian company is now general director of the Russian successor, appointed June 2023, holding a founder's stake of ~20.5%. At Berdiansk the current director of the Russian entity at the original plant address is a former deputy mayor and a known political associate of the MP whose family held pre-war ownership. Of four individuals in directing roles, three hold Ukrainian citizenship.

Why it matters

Continuity changes what the case is. A plant seized and run by outside operators is expropriation; a plant whose management stayed in place and took equity in the vehicle that acquired it is something else, with a documented ownership interest attached.

It also generalises. The method — pre-war entity, successor by address, then check the people — applies to any occupied-asset case, and it works because operational convenience beats operational security: the successor keeps the address, and often the email domain.

Individuals are anonymised by role throughout. Corporate registry identifiers are retained: companies operating in occupied territory are legitimate subjects of open-source research, and the registry numbers are the evidence. Personal-profile links have been removed.

Economies of the Aftermath

Investigation Anti-War Coalition Journal Read →

The question

Mariupol is routinely described as a city awaiting reconstruction. That framing assumes destruction is an interruption in an economy that will resume. What if the destruction is not an interruption but a precondition — and what would the physical record show?

Method

Satellite imagery analysis of the city's energy infrastructure, working from the grid rather than from buildings. Transmission lines and substations are large, fixed and identifiable from above, which makes them a harder evidentiary base than damage counts of housing stock — and they determine what an economy can physically do afterwards.

Finding

15 power transmission lines and 16 substations at 330/220/110 kV assessed across the city. What is destroyed is not only physical plant but the arrangements that let an economy function on its own terms: labour relations, market access, and the connective infrastructure between them.

Why it matters

The piece argues that such economies read better as an architecture of gaps — disrupted labour, fractured markets, severed infrastructure — than as sites awaiting reconstruction. The distinction is not rhetorical: the two readings ask different questions.

A site awaiting reconstruction invites asking when rebuilding starts. An architecture of gaps invites asking who the gaps are for — and grid capacity is where that question becomes answerable, because whoever rebuilds the substations decides what the city can produce and for whom.

At the break: Territorialization of Arctic

Investigation roflflfl.github.io Read →

The question

Accelerating ice loss is usually reported as an environmental story. It is also an access story: as the ice retreats, parts of the Arctic become physically reachable that were not before. How does Russia convert that new access into control, and is the process visible from open sources?

Method

Open-source infrastructure analysis across four case studies chosen to sit in different domains — extraction, shipping, communications and military presence — so that a common pattern across them would not be an artefact of looking at one kind of asset.

Finding

4 case studies — the Prirazlomnaya platform, the first extraction installation on the Arctic shelf; the North Sea Passage; a transarctic fibre-optic cable; and military expansion along the northern border — describe 1 process rather than four developments. Each layer is separately visible in open sources, which is what makes the whole traceable without privileged access.

Why it matters

Extraction, shipping route, data cable and garrison are four layers of a single claim, and the direction of that claim is a reorientation of Arctic spatial and economic networks toward East Asia.

Read one layer at a time, each looks like ordinary development. Read together, they are a territorial strategy — which is an argument for infrastructure analysis over incident reporting when the subject is a slow process rather than an event.

Sanctions Checker — EU · US · UK export-control lookup

Tool 2026 · TrapAggressor / StateWatch Repository →

The problem

An investigator tracking sanctions evasion has a product description in Ukrainian, or an HS code off a customs declaration, and needs to know whether it is restricted, under which instrument, since when, and where the official text says so. By hand that means reading three legal regimes — 13 EU annexes, 3 US BIS supplements, 11 UK schedules — each with its own code format and amendment history.

Approach

The obvious design is to ask a model for the HS code. It fails in the worst available way: it returns a plausible, correctly-formatted code that does not exist, and in a compliance context a confident wrong code is a false clearance. The failure is not that the model is unreliable — it is that a generated string has no source to check it against.

So the model is never asked to produce a code. It is asked for the two-digit HS chapter, a coarse classification into 97 buckets that language models do well. Every database row in that chapter is then retrieved, and the model chooses one. Its output is a selection from rows that already exist, so a code absent from the database cannot leave the pipeline.

The answer is picked, not written

Rejected query → model writes a code → no source to confirm it exists
Chapter-first query → model names a chapter → Database · every row in chapter 39 3912.11 · Annex XXIII 3912.20 · Annex XXIII 3912.31 · Annex XXI →a real row

Nothing connects the model to the answer. The row leaves the database; the model only ever named the chapter.

How it works

A FastAPI service over a SQLite database, rebuilt from official sources by 15 EU annex parsers plus UK schedule and US BIS supplement parsers. Classification runs four tiers: an alias table of roughly 200 known products, the chapter-first LLM stage above, keyword-intersection SQL with progressive relaxation, then a rapidfuzz fallback. Every answer returns its legal basis, annex, restriction type, date enacted and a deep link into the source document. It runs without the LLM, falling back to tiers three and four.

Python 3.10+FastAPISQLite → PostgreSQL pathOllama qwen2.5:7brapidfuzzpyhscodesDocker Composenginx + certbotpytest

Result

96.8% classifier accuracy on a 125-query Ukrainian-and-English test set, over 7,676 active sanctions entries — EU 2,332 · US 2,797 · UK 2,547. Roughly 7,300 lines of Python; 64 pytest tests across 3 modules, plus 5 accuracy harnesses that are how the 96.8% was measured, versioned so the number can be re-run rather than trusted.

Corpus Editor — Telegram video pipeline

Tool 2026 Repository →

The problem

A few hundred short videos scraped from Telegram are too many to watch and too unstructured to grep. Filenames and timestamps say nothing about what is in the footage, and nothing about which clips resemble each other.

Approach

Reduce every video to a vector, then let similarity do the organising — project the vectors into three dimensions and compute nearest-neighbour edges, so visually similar material sits together and the corpus can be traversed rather than searched.

The second decision matters more in practice: both expensive stages are resumable. Downloaded files are skipped on re-scrape and embedded nodes on re-process. At corpus scale a failure 80% into a run should cost the remaining 20%, not the whole run.

Per-video extraction covers ffprobe metadata, librosa audio energy, OpenCV optical-flow motion and edge complexity, MediaPipe face counts, CLIP zero-shot scene labels and k-means dominant colours, alongside the CLIP ViT-B/32 embedding itself.

It began as a tool for a film project, not an intelligence one. It is here because the pipeline underneath is the same one a media-monitoring system needs, and I would rather show one I built end to end.

Querying 77k sanctions entities — SQL and an agent

Tool 2026 Repository →

The problem

The consolidated sanctions corpus is large enough that ad-hoc questions are slow to answer and easy to answer wrongly. A natural-language layer over it is obviously attractive — and the interesting question is not whether it can be built but whether its answers can be trusted without checking the work.

Approach

Build both paths and compare them rather than assuming. One is a manual-context model: I pull data with SQL and hand the result over as text, controlling exactly what the model sees. The other is a tool-calling agent that writes its own SQL. Local models could not do tool calling reliably, so that path runs on Groq while the manual path runs on local Ollama.

How it works

A Dockerised MindsDB instance sits over the OpenSanctions consolidated dataset loaded as a table, exposing both a SQL surface and an agent surface against the same rows. Full setup from a clean machine — Docker, MindsDB, Ollama, model pull, data load and every SQL statement — is documented in the repository.

MindsDBDockerOllama gemma3:4bGroq APISQLOpenSanctions

Result

76,996 entities queryable both ways — and a documented auditability failure.

"How many sanctioned entities are from Russia?"

agent 21,210
direct SQL 19,238

1,972 records unexplainedthe agent never surfaced the query it generated

The gap is not the problem — the missing query is. Both numbers are defensible; only one is auditable.

The difference is almost certainly multi-country records like ru;ua — but that is a reconstruction after the fact. There was no way to establish which question the agent had answered without redoing the work it was meant to save. For sanctions screening that is disqualifying on its own terms: a number without its query is an assertion, not a finding. The fix is that the agent surfaces generated SQL with every answer, and the answer is a draft until that SQL has been read.

A second observation from the same session: the second-largest country group in the dataset is NULL. For anyone filtering on jurisdiction, that is a silent hole in coverage rather than an empty category.

Measuring LLM fabrication rate in OSINT profiling

Method 2026 Repository →

The problem

Analysts use language models to profile people, and everyone names hallucination as the risk. Nobody had a number for it on this kind of task — and without a number there is no way to tell whether prompt discipline actually changes the outcome or merely changes the tone.

Approach

Run the same profiling task twice and vary exactly one thing. Same model, same subject, and search explicitly disabled on both sides — that last part matters, because my first attempt compared a tool with retrieval against one without and was therefore not a prompt comparison at all. Then extract every discrete claim and check them one at a time.

How it works

Twelve claims were extracted from the unconstrained output and each scored true, hallucinated, unverifiable or partial, with the basis recorded per claim against three independent source families. "Unverifiable" is kept as its own category rather than folded into true or false, because collapsing it in either direction is precisely how unsourced claims enter finished analysis.

Unconstrained prompt · 12 claims

4 unverifiable
2 fabricated
6 confirmed or partial

Source-constrained, same task — nothing fabricated, nothing unverifiable

The unverifiable band is the heaviest element because it is the argument: a fabrication can be caught by checking, an unfalsifiable claim cannot be checked at all.

Result

The unconstrained prompt produced 17% explicit hallucination and 33% unverifiable claims. The source-constrained prompt on the identical task produced 0% fabrication.

What moved the number was not the persona framing or the tone instruction but two clauses — use only facts explicitly present in the text and if a field is absent, return null — which change the model's implicit objective from producing a complete-looking profile to reporting what the source supports.

The most instructive error was not the fabrication rate. The model placed the subject at the wrong university, and the university it named is a real institution in the same city: locally plausible, superficially correct, invisible without a deliberate check. A prompt-injection test alongside was detected and refused unprompted — a narrower result than "models are safe", since one passing test on one model is not a control.

Scope and limits

Sample
One subject, twelve claims. An order-of-magnitude signal about a failure mode, not a benchmark — the method transfers, the exact numbers do not
Subject
Anonymised: a serving regional official in Ukraine. Identifying source URLs removed; source types named so the method stays reproducible
Scoring
"Unverified" means no public source was located either way
Reproduction
Model versions were not pinned at the time of the run

Russia Context Platform

Research · unfinished 2026

The problem

Detecting a synthetic persona means knowing what a convincing one would have to get right. That list is not published anywhere. Detection work consequently runs on intuition about which signals are hard to fake — and intuition is a poor basis for deciding where to spend a detector's attention.

Approach

Build the substrate for a single Russian settlement and see how far it gets. Collect the local Telegram channels, regional RSS and VK material for that one place, then synthesise a place profile from it: local geography, institutions, transport, prices, seasonal rhythms, recurring names, register and dialect — deep enough that a generated resident would be hard for an actual resident to catch out.

Working at settlement scale rather than national scale is the whole point. National context is abundant and cheap; it is the local, unrecorded texture that is expensive, and expense is exactly what a detector wants to test.

How it works

A collection layer pulls from regional sources on a schedule and normalises what comes back. Synthesis runs over that corpus into a structured place profile. The primary store is a wiki rather than a database — a deliberate choice: the material is mostly prose with citations, it is edited by hand as often as by machine, and a wiki keeps provenance and revision attached to each claim in a way a schema does not. A knowledge graph sits alongside it for entities and relations, with a map layer for the geographic material. Models run locally, since the corpus is regional-language material that does not need to leave the machine.

It is unfinished. Collection and the wiki store work; the graph is partial, the map layer is early, and the synthesis step has been run over a small number of settlements rather than at any scale.

Result

5 signal classes, ranked by how hard each turned out to fabricate — which is the output that transfers to detection.

What must be faked · what a detector must check

Signal classDetection surface
Place knowledge→detail finer than public maps carry
Temporal habits→posting rhythm against local hours
Dialect, register→lexis against regional corpora
Social graph→age and reciprocity of ties, not their count
Event memory→recall of what no source ever wrote down

The first four are approximable from collected material. Event memory is not — which makes it the cheapest thing for a defender to test and the most expensive for an adversary to sustain.

The four upper classes turned out to be tractable: enough local geography, rhythm, lexis and plausible social structure can be assembled from open regional sources to survive casual review. Event memory did not. A profile built from sources can only contain what a source recorded, and the unrecorded local event — the thing everyone in a town knows and nobody wrote down — has no substitute in the corpus.

For detection that inverts the usual priority. Effort is better spent probing for recall of unrecorded specifics than on stylometry or posting-time analysis, and coherence across classes matters more than any single anomaly. It also sets a realistic ceiling: a detector built on the assumption of sloppy synthesis will not catch competent synthesis, and competent synthesis is achievable with open sources.

Unfinished and not open-sourced. There is no repository, no code, no screenshots and no operational parameters here, and there will not be. Describing what a thing is and handing someone a working copy are different acts, and only the second one matters for this project.
